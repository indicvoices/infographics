A parameter-by-parameter scorecard · India & China, 2026

Ballot vs. Delivery: who actually governs for its people?

India votes. China delivers. Neither claim survives first contact with the evidence untouched — so this piece scores both countries, one substantive parameter at a time, sourced throughout.

Every five years, roughly 970 million Indians are handed an inked finger and told this is democracy. But casting a ballot and having a government that is actually accountable to you are not the same thing — and conflating them is how a country can hold elections for seventy-five years while its politics remains dominated by the same families, the same money, and the same manufactured divisions.

The case for treating voting and democracy as separate questions starts with a simple observation: a system in which political parties are structurally dependent on capital, and voters are structurally excluded from evaluating performance between elections, does not automatically produce accountable government just because a vote is held. India spent an official ₹60,000 crore running the 2019 Lok Sabha election — and that's just the disclosed figure. Each candidate was capped at ₹95 lakh in campaign spending, a cap widely understood to be fictional. That kind of money doesn't come free. It is fronted by industrialists and party financiers, and it is recovered — through access, through contracts, through the silence of regulators, and, when that isn't enough, through outright scams. When money bankrolls both the winning and the losing side, as it usually does, then whoever wins, it's their candidate who wins — not the voter's.

The deeper problem is structural: voters don't actually choose their representatives — they choose between two or three pre-selected slates, assembled by parties that answer to their financiers first. There is no ongoing evaluation. We elect. We don't evaluate. Once a candidate wins, there is no mechanism to demote, retrain, or remove them for non-performance short of waiting five years and hoping the alternative is better — while the same career politicians, the same dynasties, and often the same criminal defendants rotate between parties every election cycle. And the entry price for even attempting this is prohibitive: 46% of the winning candidates in the 2024 Lok Sabha have declared criminal cases against them, and 93% are crorepatis — a system that filters for wealth and impunity before it filters for competence.

A framing note before the scorecard. Most standard democracy indices (V-Dem, EIU, Freedom House) weight procedural democracy heavily — competitive multi-party elections, judicial independence, peaceful transfer of power — and by that yardstick India scores far above China, which has no national multi-party elections and no legal opposition. This piece deliberately uses a different, substantive yardstick instead: does government actually work for ordinary citizens, and can they hold it accountable in any real sense? That's a legitimate and defensible lens — but it isn't the only one, and where the two lenses genuinely conflict (peaceful transfer of power, judicial independence, media freedom), this piece says so rather than quietly picking whichever framing favours one side.

How this scorecard works

  1. 13 parameters, each scored 0–10 for India and for China, based on the most recent hard data available — government statistics, UN/World Bank/WHO datasets, and independent indices (Transparency International, Freedom House, RSF, CPJ, WJP, Edelman) — plus documented case examples.
  2. Every parameter shows both sides of the argument before the score, including the strongest case against the position this piece ultimately takes.
  3. Sources are linked under each parameter. Where indices disagree or data is contested, that's flagged rather than smoothed over.
  4. The final score is a simple, unweighted average of all 13 parameters — no parameter is given extra weight to push the conclusion in either direction.

Case for China

Adult literacy stands around 97%, against India's ~76–77% — a gap of two full decades in developmental terms.

Under-5 child mortality is ~7 per 1,000 live births versus India's ~28 per 1,000 — India's rate is roughly four times higher.

Childhood stunting sits at 4.8% and wasting at 1.9% in China; India's NFHS-5 survey puts stunting at 35.5% and wasting at 19.3% — among the highest child-malnutrition burdens in the world for a country that isn't at war or in famine.

China built the world's largest high-speed rail network (over 45,000 km) in under two decades, largely on schedule, alongside near-universal electrification and sanitation.

Case for India

India's numbers are improving, not static: stunting fell from 38.4% to 35.5% and wasting from 21.0% to 19.3% between NFHS-4 and NFHS-5 — real, if slow, progress.

India delivers this under vastly harder starting conditions: a more diverse, federal, and poorer starting point in 1950 than China had after 1949, plus a democratic requirement to build consensus across 28 states with veto power China's unitary system doesn't need.

Comparisons of authoritarian versus democratic delivery generally ignore that authoritarian data is self-reported by the same government being measured — China's own statistics bureau has admitted to provincial GDP overstatement in the past, and independent verification of health data is harder to obtain than in India, where NFHS surveys are conducted with international (DHS/ICF) technical partners.

Score rationale: Even generously discounting for data-opacity concerns, the gap between China and India on basic human-development delivery — literacy, child survival, malnutrition — is not a rounding error; it is one of the largest such gaps between any two large economies in the world. This is the parameter where the "electoral democracy ≠ delivery" argument is strongest.

Case for China

CPC officials typically enter through a tiered, examination-and-performance-based cadre system: village/township administration, then county, prefecture, province, and only after decades of documented performance, the centre. It is a closed system, but a functioning promotion ladder — not a wealth or celebrity filter.

A large share of top Chinese leadership has technical or engineering backgrounds and administrative track records; there is no equivalent in China of a film star or cricketer being fielded for a national seat with zero governance experience.

Case for India

46% of winning 2024 Lok Sabha candidates have declared criminal cases (31% "serious" — including rape, murder, kidnapping); 93% are crorepatis, with average declared assets of ₹46.34 crore per winning MP. A candidate with a criminal case had more than 3x the winning probability (15.3%) of a clean candidate (4.4%).

Dynastic capture is not a Congress problem alone — it runs across the spectrum: DMK (Karunanidhi/Stalin), Samajwadi Party (Yadav), NCP (Pawar), Shiv Sena (Thackeray), National Conference (Abdullah), TDP (Naidu), RJD (Lalu/Tejashwi) — and BJP itself routinely fields film and sports celebrities with no apprenticeship, alongside allies drawn from the same dynastic pool.

China's own "meritocracy" is heavily gated by factional patronage and princeling networks (children of former senior leaders) — Xi Jinping himself is the son of a Politburo veteran. Entry is closed to anyone outside the party; there is no path in from civil society, media, or opposition at all, because there is no legal opposition.

Score rationale: China's internal promotion ladder is a genuine, if opaque and non-competitive-in-the-electoral-sense, alternative to dynastic and money politics. India's entry system currently filters for wealth and criminal impunity ahead of competence, across nearly every major party. China does not escape criticism here — it simply trades one closed elite (capital + dynasty) for another (party + princelings) — but the electoral-cost barrier and criminalization data make India's version measurably worse on this specific axis.

Media freedom & freedom of information

Press independence · journalist imprisonment · internet censorship
China
1.0
India
4.0
0246810

Case for India

RSF's 2026 World Press Freedom Index ranks India 157th of 180 — a real and worsening problem, driven by concentrated media ownership and government-aligned outlets. But China ranks 178th of 180, ahead of only North Korea and Eritrea.

India has no Great Firewall equivalent — Indians can access foreign press, social media, and VPNs (with some state-level exceptions during unrest); China blocks Google, Facebook, X, most foreign news sites, and Wikipedia outright, nationwide, permanently.

CPJ's 2024–25 prison census found China jailing 50 journalists — the world's single worst offender for three consecutive years — versus 2–3 journalists in Indian custody.

Case for China

India's fall from 142nd (2021) to 157th (2026) is a genuinely alarming trajectory, and press freedom groups now describe India's largest news channels as functioning as government amplifiers rather than a check on power — a "very serious" category that most working definitions of democracy would treat as disqualifying, not incidental.

Internet shutdowns are far more frequent in India than in China in raw count — India has led the world in state-ordered internet shutdowns for several consecutive years (mostly regional/local, concentrated around protests and Kashmir), even though China's blocking is more total and permanent in scope.

Score rationale: Both systems are compromised. But the difference in kind, not just degree, matters: China's censorship is total, permanent, and constitutionally unchallengeable; India's is a serious and worsening capture of a nominally free press that citizens can still — and do — publicly criticize, litigate against, and vote to change. This is one of the parameters where the user's own framework (weighing dissent lower than delivery) cuts against China rather than for it.

Right to dissent & civil liberties in practice

Not just what's on paper — what actually happens to dissenters on the ground
China
1.0
India
5.0
0246810

Case for India

Freedom House scores India 66/100 ("Partly Free") — a real decline from "Free" status in 2021, driven by rising violence and discriminatory policy affecting Muslims, crackdowns on media, academics, and protesters. China scores 9/100 ("Not Free") — one of the very lowest scores of any country measured, worse than most authoritarian states.

India's specific abuses are documented and severe — the arrests of Muslim youths for breaking an Eid fast with meat near religious sites in Varanasi, the jailing of journalists and activists under sedition-adjacent laws (UAPA), and disproportionate state action against critics are real and belong in this conversation.

Case for China

India's own record includes activists and journalists held for months on charges that rarely result in conviction — a form of process-as-punishment that critics say is functionally repressive even without a formal "not free" designation.

China's supporters would argue that a system with no illusions of dissent-tolerance at least doesn't claim a right it doesn't honour — though this is closer to a defence of consistency than of the underlying practice.

Score rationale: This is the parameter the user flagged as likely to favour India even while sympathizing with the "Indian democracy is compromised in practice" argument — and the evidence bears that out. India's abuses are real, serious, and rising, but citizens can and do organize protests, litigate against the state, and win in court with some regularity. In China, none of that is structurally possible.
Sources
  • Freedom House — Freedom in the World 2025, China: 9/100, "Not Free": freedomhouse.org

Punishment of corruption

Perceived corruption levels · conviction and enforcement record
China
7.0
India
3.0
0246810

Case for China

Transparency International's CPI 2024 scores China 43/100 (rank 76 of 180) versus India's 38/100 (rank 96 of 180, down from 93rd).

China's anti-corruption drive since 2012 has disciplined over a million officials and produced a small number of capital sentences for the largest cases — e.g. former China Huarong chairman Lai Xiaomin, executed in 2021 after a $12.7bn bribery conviction. Whatever one thinks of the severity, it demonstrates enforcement capacity India's system conspicuously lacks against politically connected offenders.

Case for India

India's scam-to-consequence pipeline is well documented and close to zero: the Sarada chit-fund scam (₹10,000+ crore, 2013) and the West Bengal SSC recruitment scam both produced arrests of mid-level operatives while senior political figures implicated returned to prominent party positions with no lasting consequence.

China's anti-corruption campaign is also credibly used as a factional weapon — a tool for purging Xi's rivals as much as fighting graft, with no independent judiciary or free press to check whether prosecutions are politically selective. A high conviction rate under one-party control with no adversarial defence and no independent media is a different, and less trustworthy, kind of "enforcement" than the CPI score alone conveys.

Score rationale: On outcomes — does the perpetrator actually face consequences — China's record, even discounted for its use as a political weapon, exceeds India's near-total impunity for senior, politically connected corruption. India's CPI score has been declining, not improving, over the past two years.

Religion and politics

State patronage of religious figures and movements vs. official secularism
China
6.0
India
3.0
0246810

Case for China

Indian political life is saturated with state patronage of self-styled godmen — figures like Bageshwar Baba, Ramdev, and Aniruddhacharya routinely appear alongside senior ministers, receive land grants and platforms, and in some cases wield informal influence over policy or public health messaging (Ramdev's Patanjali business empire has repeatedly clashed with — and influenced — drug-regulatory politics). No equivalent figure has comparable proximity to the CPC leadership; officially, Party members are required to be atheist, and religious figures are kept structurally separate from governance.

Case for India

China's "secularism" is not neutrality — it is aggressive state control and suppression of religion, which is a different and arguably worse problem than India's patronage model. Falun Gong practitioners have faced mass detention; underground Christian churches are regularly demolished; and the treatment of Muslim and Buddhist minorities (covered in Parameter 11) goes far beyond "keeping religion out of politics" into state-directed religious persecution.

India's constitution enshrines secularism and freedom of religion as fundamental rights, and — however imperfectly enforced against godman patronage — has no equivalent of forced re-education camps or banned scriptures.

Score rationale: Narrowly scoped to this parameter's actual question — does the ruling party entangle itself with religious figures for political gain, or keep religion structurally separate from governance — China's model scores better than India's godman patronage. But this parameter deliberately excludes religious-freedom and minority-persecution questions, which are scored separately in Parameter 11, precisely because folding them together would let China's score here obscure a far worse record elsewhere.
Sources
  • Widely documented pattern of BJP, Congress and regional-party leaders publicly associating with commercial godmen; see ongoing reporting by The Wire, Scroll.in, and Article 14 on Bageshwar Baba, Ramdev/Patanjali regulatory episodes, and similar figures.

Edelman Trust Barometer — how much citizens say they trust their own government
China
8.3
India
7.9
0246810

Case for China

The 2025 Edelman Trust Barometer records China at 83% trust in government, the highest or near-highest of any country surveyed, consistent with prior years (China topped the overall Trust Index at 77 in the same survey). This is corroborated across multiple independent academic surveys (e.g. Harvard Ash Center longitudinal studies going back to 2003) showing consistently high and rising satisfaction with the central government specifically.

Case for India

India isn't far behind at 79% — among the very highest in the world, well above the US, UK, Germany, France, or Japan. The gap here is real but modest, and complicates the "surprisingly, Chinese citizens trust their government more than Indians" framing: both are unusually high-trust societies compared to Western democracies, for reasons that likely include genuine delivery, media environment, and social-desirability bias in survey responses under different degrees of political openness.

Trust surveys inside authoritarian states are also methodologically fraught — the "spiral of silence" effect means citizens under closer surveillance may be less willing to report distrust to any interviewer, government-linked or not, inflating the reported number in ways harder to correct for than in India's freer survey environment.

Score rationale: China leads, but narrowly — not by the wide margin the "citizens can tell the difference" framing implies. Given the methodological caveats about surveying trust under an authoritarian system, this parameter is scored as a close call rather than a decisive one.
Sources
  • Edelman 2025 Trust Barometer — global press release, China/India Trust Index: edelman.com

Economic mobility & inequality outcomes

Poverty reduction pace · inequality trends
China
7.0
India
5.0
0246810

Case for China

China lifted roughly 800 million people out of extreme poverty between 1981 and 2020 — about 74% of all global poverty reduction in that period — and declared elimination of extreme poverty (at the $2.15/day line) by 2020.

Case for India

India's recent trajectory is genuinely strong and under-credited: extreme poverty (at $2.15/day) fell from 16.2% to 2.3% between 2011-12 and 2022-23 — 171 million people lifted out of extreme poverty in a single decade, per the World Bank's 2025 brief, with the rural-urban poverty gap narrowing sharply.

China's Gini coefficient of income inequality rose far more sharply during its growth period (from ~29 to ~42) than India's (~31 to ~33 by comparable measures), meaning China's growth, while faster at eliminating absolute poverty, has been noticeably less equally shared — a real tension in a "delivers for its citizens" argument if citizens means all of them, not just those who moved fastest.

At the higher, more realistic $6.85/day poverty line, more Indians are poor today than in 1990 in absolute terms (due to population growth), a caveat the World Bank itself flags and that complicates any triumphalist reading of either country's numbers.

Score rationale: China's historical achievement in eliminating absolute poverty at scale is larger and faster than India's, but India's last decade of progress is real, recent, and often overlooked — and China's much sharper rise in inequality is a genuine cost the "efficient delivery" argument has to reckon with, not wave away.

Peaceful transfer of power

Institutional turnover norms vs. genuinely contestable elections
China
4.0
India
6.0
0246810

Case for China

Deng Xiaoping's post-Mao two-term/10-year norm produced genuinely smooth, institutionalized top-leadership turnover for 25 years — Jiang Zemin (1993–2003) and Hu Jintao (2003–2013) both stepped down on schedule, a discipline India's dynastic and incumbency patterns (Nehru ~17 years; Modi now 11+ years and counting, no term limit on the office) haven't matched.

Case for India

That norm was never a democratic mechanism — it was an unwritten elite consensus with no public vote and no institution empowered to enforce it, which is precisely why Xi Jinping could get it abolished in 2018 through a single National People's Congress vote (2,958–2, with 3 abstentions) with zero public input and no possible avenue of citizen resistance.

India's incumbents, however long-serving, have repeatedly lost power through contested elections: Indira Gandhi lost in 1977 (directly after the Emergency, to public backlash), Vajpayee's coalition lost in 2004, Congress lost in 2014. No CCP leader has ever faced removal by a citizen vote at any level of top leadership — succession has meant transfer within the party elite, never transfer of power away from the party. Meanwhile, India's use of central agencies (ED/CBI) against opposition politicians — several of whom switched to the ruling party shortly after being raided — is a real and separate concern about electoral fairness, but it operates alongside a mechanism that can still remove an incumbent, which China's system structurally cannot allow at all.

Score rationale: A norm one man can unilaterally erase by fiat is a different category of thing from an institutional check citizens can invoke. China gets credit for 25 years of orderly elite succession; India gets credit for a mechanism — however compromised in its current execution — that has demonstrably removed sitting governments within living memory and remains legally available to do so again.
Sources
  • 2018 NPC vote abolishing China's presidential term limits (2,958–2, 3 abstentions) — widely reported at the time by Reuters, BBC, and Xinhua's own official record.

Judicial independence & rule of law

World Justice Project Rule of Law Index
China
3.0
India
6.0
0246810

Case for India

The WJP Rule of Law Index 2024 ranks India 79th of 142 countries; China ranks 92nd of 143 — China trails India on the composite measure. India's judiciary, despite serious backlogs and delay, regularly rules against the government — striking down laws, granting bail to high-profile dissidents, and hearing challenges to executive action. China's courts operate under explicit Party supervision (the "Political-Legal Committee" system) with no comparable independence.

Case for China

India's own WJP sub-scores are weak on exactly the dimensions that matter most for ordinary citizens: it ranks 102nd on fundamental rights protection and 82nd on criminal justice efficiency — India's courts have a backlog exceeding 50 million pending cases, meaning "judicial independence" often translates to years or decades of undertrial detention rather than timely justice, a form of denial-by-delay that is its own kind of injustice.

China's commercial and civil justice systems (as distinct from politically sensitive cases) are actually fast and increasingly well-regarded by international business for contract enforcement — WJP notes China's civil justice did not weaken even as most countries' did in 2024.

Score rationale: On the specific question of judicial independence — can courts constrain the executive — India's judiciary, however slow and imperfect, does this in practice with some regularity; China's cannot, by design. This is a parameter where India's procedural strengths translate into a real substantive protection its citizens can and do use.

1.0

4.0

Case for India (relatively)

The UN's Office of the High Commissioner for Human Rights has assessed that Xinjiang detentions of Uyghurs and other Turkic Muslims — independently estimated at over 1 million people at peak — "may constitute international crimes, in particular crimes against humanity." No credible international finding of comparable severity or scale exists for any Indian minority policy.

India's abuses are real and documented — the arrest of Muslim youths for breaking Eid fast with meat near religious sites in Varanasi, the events surrounding CAA/NRC, and disproportionate policing of Muslim communities during communal violence — but they occur within a legal and media environment where they can be reported, litigated, and publicly protested, however imperfectly.

Case for China (relatively)

India's treatment of minorities cannot be waved away as merely "isolated incidents" — human rights monitors have documented a rise in hate-speech prosecutions being selectively applied, bulldozer demolitions targeting Muslim-owned properties after unrest, and a communal climate that independent India scholars describe as the worst since Partition. Kashmir's post-2019 status, with prolonged internet shutdowns, detentions, and restricted political representation, is itself a serious and ongoing case that international rights bodies have separately criticized.

Score rationale: Both records are genuinely serious and deserve to be named without minimizing either. But treating them as equivalent would itself be a distortion: the scale, documentation, and international legal characterization of Xinjiang (mass internment, forced labour allegations, demographic engineering) sits in a different category of severity than anything currently documented in India, even accounting for India's real and worsening record.
Sources

Grassroots governance & gender representation

Panchayati Raj reserved seats vs. China's near-total exclusion of women from top leadership
China
4.0
India
6.0
0246810

Case for India

The 73rd Constitutional Amendment (1993) constitutionally reserves at least one-third of all directly elected Panchayati Raj seats (village to district level) for women, including chairperson posts — over 21 states have since raised this to 50%. These are real constitutional bodies with taxation, planning, and welfare-oversight powers affecting hundreds of millions. India has also had a female Prime Minister, President, and multiple state Chief Ministers.

China's Politburo Standing Committee — the CCP's supreme decision-making body — has never once had a female member in the history of the People's Republic. The current 24-member Politburo has just one or two women; women hold under 9% of Central Committee seats.

Case for China

China's National People's Congress — the larger national legislature — has women at roughly 25% of seats, more than double the Lok Sabha's ~14%. At the sheer numbers level, more Chinese women hold some elected office nationally than Indian women do, even if China's apex leadership is more male-dominated than India's.

Implementation gaps undercut India's constitutional promise in practice — "sarpanch pati" arrangements, where an elected woman's husband or male relative actually exercises the power while she serves as a proxy, are a widely documented problem across many panchayats, meaning formal representation doesn't always translate to real authority.

Score rationale: India's structural advantage here is real and constitutionally locked in at the level that touches the most people directly (local government), even though implementation is imperfect and national-level representation lags. China's larger legislature has more women in raw numbers, but its actual centres of power remain almost entirely closed to women by any measure — a gap that has, if anything, worsened since 2022, when the Politburo dropped to zero female members for the first time since 2002.

Environmental & public health governance

Pollution response speed — Beijing vs. Delhi
China
8.0
India
4.0
0246810

Case for China

Beijing's PM2.5 fell by roughly 40–50% between 2013 and 2021–23, backed by a $100bn, decade-long enforcement plan: coal-to-gas conversion, factory relocation, real-time emissions monitoring, and penalties with actual teeth (China's power-plant particulate standard is 10 mg/m³, versus India's 30–100 mg/m³ — three to ten times looser).

Delhi's PM2.5, by contrast, has shown essentially no sustained improvement over the same period — down roughly 3% since 2015 — despite recurring "airpocalypse" emergencies, school closures, and construction bans every winter.

Case for India

Delhi faces structural problems Beijing never had to solve — most importantly stubble burning in neighbouring states, which China's more centralized administrative geography doesn't replicate — plus a fragmented governance structure spanning the Central Pollution Control Board, Delhi Pollution Control Committee, and Commission for Air Quality Management, with jurisdictional overlap that a unitary Chinese system doesn't have to navigate.

China's own reporting transparency has been independently questioned; Delhi at least publishes AQI data local researchers can and do independently criticize as flawed, which is itself a form of accountability China's more centrally-managed reporting doesn't allow for.

Score rationale: This is one of the clearest "delivery" parameters in the entire scorecard: Beijing set a target, enforced it against powerful industrial interests, and hit it within a decade. Delhi has cycled through the same crisis-response rituals for over a decade with negligible measured improvement — a governance failure that costs an estimated 17,000+ lives a year in Delhi alone.

The honest result is closer than either a "China is obviously more democratic" or "India is obviously more democratic" framing would predict — and that's the point of scoring parameters independently instead of picking a conclusion first. China leads decisively on delivery, corruption enforcement, and environmental governance — precisely the substantive terrain this piece opened by arguing matters more than the ritual of voting. India leads clearly on media freedom, dissent, judicial independence, peaceful transfer of power, minority treatment, and grassroots representation — the terrain most standard definitions of democracy actually measure.

What the numbers don't support is the strong version of the claim that China "has more democracy" in any sense most people would recognize the word to mean — no multi-party elections, no free press, no independent judiciary, and a security-state response to Xinjiang that international bodies have called a possible crime against humanity are not incidental features of the substantive-delivery argument; they are costs of the same concentrated, unaccountable power that makes the delivery possible. What the numbers do support is a narrower, more defensible claim: that India's electoral rituals have not produced the accountability its founders promised, and that a government can hold elections every five years while still failing, in concrete and measurable ways, to work for the people who voted for it. That's a claim India can act on without needing China's political system as a model — the 73rd Amendment's Panchayati Raj reservations, this scorecard's own strongest example of accountable local delivery, is proof India's constitution already contains better tools than it currently uses.
