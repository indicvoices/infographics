Vulgar & Threatening Language Aimed at U.S. Presidents

A timeline of notable profane, insulting, or violent-sounding remarks directed at sitting presidents — and what actually happened legally to the people who said them.

The general pattern: Under U.S. law, threatening a president's life is a federal crime (18 U.S.C. § 871). But since Watts v. United States (1969), the Supreme Court has drawn a firm line between a "true threat" and political hyperbole, insult, or artistic expression — which is protected by the First Amendment. That's why almost all of the profanity below produced Secret Service attention at most, but no charges.
1960s–1970s — The "True Threat" Standard Is Born

Robert Watts Lyndon B. Johnson "If they ever make me carry a rifle, the first man I want to get in my sights is L.B.J." Anti-draft rally near the Washington Monument (Aug. 1966); crowd laughed after the remark. Yes Convicted under federal threat law; Supreme Court unanimously overturned it in Watts v. United States (1969) — ruled "crude political hyperbole," not a true threat. This case still sets the legal standard.
Anti-war protesters Nixon "Fuck Nixon" Vietnam War-era protests, on signs and chants. No Protected political speech.

2017–2021 — The Trump Presidency (First Term)

Protesters (various) Donald Trump "Fuck Trump" Anti-Trump protests, signs, flags, merchandise. No Protected political speech; no arrests for the slogan itself.
Madonna Donald Trump "Yes, I have thought an awful lot about blowing up the White House... but I know that this won't change anything." 2017 Women's March speech. No Secret Service said it was aware of the remark; no charges after Madonna called it a metaphor "taken wildly out of context." Some conservatives (e.g. Newt Gingrich) publicly called for her arrest.
Madonna Donald Trump Improvised closing line of "Express Yourself," telling Trump to perform oral sex on a man. Same 2017 Women's March performance, immediately after her speech. No No legal action; part of the same broader controversy over her set.
Stephen Colbert Donald Trump Joked that the only use for Trump's mouth was performing oral sex on Vladimir Putin. "Late Show" monologue, May 1, 2017, responding to Trump insulting a CBS journalist. No No legal action. Sparked a #FireColbert boycott campaign and an FCC review after viewer complaints; the FCC ultimately cleared the joke and CBS took no action against him.
Johnny Depp Donald Trump "When was the last time an actor assassinated a president? ... it's been a while and maybe it's time." 2017 Glastonbury Festival, introducing a screening of "The Libertine." No Secret Service confirmed it was aware of the comment; no charges. Depp later apologized, calling it "a bad joke ... in poor taste."
Kathy Griffin Donald Trump Photo holding a fake severed, bloodied Trump head. 2017 photo shoot with photographer Tyler Shields. No Secret Service investigated; no charges filed. She was dropped from CNN's New Year's Eve broadcast and lost sponsorships/tour dates amid backlash.
Snoop Dogg Donald Trump Music video depicting a clown resembling Trump being "shot" with a toy gun. "Lavender" music video (2017). No No criminal action; Trump criticized it publicly on Twitter.
Eminem Donald Trump "Fuck Donald Trump" Freestyle ("The Storm") at the 2017 BET Hip Hop Awards. No No legal action; treated as protected artistic/political expression.
Robert De Niro Donald Trump "Fuck Trump!" Introducing Bruce Springsteen at the 2018 Tony Awards. No No legal action; treated as political speech (CBS bleeped the broadcast).
Samantha Bee Ivanka Trump (WH adviser) Called Ivanka Trump a "feckless c—" and told her to tell her father to "f—ing stop it," over family-separation immigration policy. 2018 episode of "Full Frontal with Samantha Bee," commenting on a photo Ivanka posted with her son. No No legal consequences. Bee apologized publicly; at least 13 advertisers pulled ads from the show, and Trump himself tweeted calling for her to be fired.
Bette Midler Donald Trump Repeated social-media mockery of Trump's physical appearance and masculinity. Ongoing Twitter/X posts. No No legal action; protected political expression.
